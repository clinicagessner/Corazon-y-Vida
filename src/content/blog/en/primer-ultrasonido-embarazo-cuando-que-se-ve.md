Marisol took a drugstore test on a Saturday morning, saw two lines, and the first thing she told her partner was, "I want to see it now." That impulse is completely normal. After a positive test, an ultrasound is what makes the news feel real. But the first pregnancy ultrasound is more than a picture for the family group chat: it answers specific medical questions, and timing it well spares you a lot of unnecessary worry. Here the clinic's medical team explains what this scan is for, when it usually makes sense, and what it can't settle on its own.

## Three things the first scan tells you

A urine or blood test shows that pregnancy hormone (hCG) is present. It can't tell you where the pregnancy is growing or how far along it is. That's the job of an early ultrasound:

1. **Is it inside the uterus?** This is the first thing checked. A pregnancy that implants outside the uterus, usually in a fallopian tube, can't continue and can put the mother in danger.
2. **How many weeks along?** Measuring the embryo dates the pregnancy more precisely than your last period does, especially if your cycles are irregular or you aren't sure when your period started. That measurement is used to set the due date.
3. **Is there a heartbeat, and how many babies?** Seeing cardiac activity is one of the most reassuring signs of the early weeks. It's also the scan where a lot of families find out they're expecting twins.

At the clinic, the pregnancy [ultrasound](/services/ultrasonido) covers exactly that: confirmation, gestational age, heartbeat and, later on, growth follow-up. What appears on the screen is explained to you in Spanish.

## Why going too early can scare you for no reason

Plenty of women want the scan the same weekend the test turns positive. The catch is that in the very first weeks the pregnancy may be too small to see yet, or only the sac shows with no embryo inside. That doesn't mean something is wrong; often it just means it's early. Weeks are counted from the first day of your last period, not from the date you conceived. As a general guide (every pregnancy and every machine differ), here is what a transvaginal scan typically shows:

| Weeks since your last period | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| Around 5 | A small gestational sac in the uterus, sometimes nothing yet |
| Around 6 | The start of the embryo and, often, the first flicker of a heartbeat |
| 7 to 9 | An embryo that can be measured and a clearer heartbeat; a good window for dating |
| 11 to 13 | A fetus with a more defined shape that is already moving |

So if your period is only a few days late and you have no pain or bleeding, it's often worth waiting a little before that first scan. When it's done very early and nothing is visible, the usual plan is to repeat it in a week or two or to compare hormone levels in your blood, and that limbo tends to be harder than simply waiting from the start.

## Through the belly or transvaginal?

Early on, the uterus is still tucked behind the pubic bone. That's why in the first weeks a transvaginal scan (a slim, covered, lubricated probe placed a few centimeters into the vagina) usually gives a much sharper picture than one done over the belly. It isn't painful; most women describe a pressure similar to a pelvic exam, and it does not harm the pregnancy.

The clinic's ultrasound page notes that the pelvic study is done through the abdomen or transvaginally depending on the case, and the prep is different for each: an early-pregnancy scan over the belly calls for a full bladder (about four glasses of water an hour before, without urinating), while a transvaginal scan needs an empty one. Ask which one you're getting before you drink all that water. You can also ask for your partner or another woman to be in the room.

## When the heartbeat "isn't there"

This is the moment that causes the most anxiety. If you're told the heartbeat can't be seen yet, ask three things: how many weeks the pregnancy measures, whether the sac is inside the uterus, and what happens next. Often the explanation is that you're less far along than you thought because you ovulated late. Other times a quantitative [blood pregnancy test](/services/prueba-embarazo) is needed to see whether the hormone is rising as expected, followed by a second scan. Not seeing it today isn't a final answer; follow-up is what clears things up.

## What the first scan doesn't do

A good early ultrasound is very reassuring, but it's just one piece of your pregnancy care:

- **It doesn't replace prenatal care.** Prenatal visits track blood pressure, weight, blood and urine tests, vitamins and medications, and they change as the pregnancy moves along. One scan can't stand in for that.
- **It isn't the anatomy scan.** The detailed look at the baby's organs generally happens around the middle of pregnancy, in a separate ultrasound.
- **It can't tell boy from girl yet.** At this stage the genitals can't be told apart; that question will have to wait for later scans.
- **It isn't a keepsake session.** Ultrasound uses no radiation and is safe in pregnancy, but it's done when it adds medical information; scans just to get a picture, with no medical reason, aren't recommended.

## When not to wait for any ultrasound

Some symptoms aren't a reason to book a visit. They're a reason to go straight to the emergency room or call 911, whether or not you've had a scan:

- Severe pain on one side of your belly, especially if it keeps getting worse.
- Heavy bleeding that soaks a pad or more in an hour, or passes large clots.
- Intense dizziness, fainting, or feeling like you're about to pass out.
- Pain at the tip of your shoulder along with a positive test.

The clinic's pregnancy test page is clear on this: these signs can point to a pregnancy outside the uterus and need emergency care right away. Light spotting without pain is common early on, but mention it to the medical team soon.

## Coming in with a positive test

Picture a common situation: Yesenia, 34, has high blood pressure and takes a pill every day. Her home test came back positive and she didn't know whether to keep taking it. For someone like her, the first visit matters as much as the scan. Here's what it usually looks like:

1. The test is confirmed in urine or, if there's any doubt, in blood.
2. Your weeks are estimated from your last period, and your blood pressure and weight are checked.
3. Your medications are reviewed: some for blood pressure, acne or cholesterol need to be changed. If you live with diabetes, high blood pressure or a thyroid problem, the follow-up for those [chronic conditions](/services/condiciones-cronicas) is adjusted from the start.
4. You get folic acid and guidance on prenatal care.
5. When the location or age of the pregnancy needs confirming, the clinic's medical team orders the ultrasound, and no appointment is needed for it.

Bring the first day of your last period written down, a list of the medications and vitamins you take, and the home test if you kept it. For gynecology and prenatal checkups in the months ahead, ask about the [women's health visit](/services/ginecologia).
